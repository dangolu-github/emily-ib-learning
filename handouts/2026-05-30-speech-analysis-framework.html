0530 Exam Prep · Speech Analysis Framework

Analyzing a Speech in Paper 1

0. Contextual & Situational Elements

The rhetorical situation:

  • Speaker — Who is speaking? What authority/credibility do they hold?

  • Audience — Immediate audience vs. broader/secondary audience (e.g., televised viewers, posterity)

  • Occasion/Kairos — The timing and circumstances; why this moment matters

  • Purpose — To persuade, commemorate, mobilize, console, condemn, inspire?

  • Medium — Live address, broadcast, ceremonial, parliamentary, etc.

* Genre-Specific Speech Conventions

  • Epideictic — Ceremonial (eulogies, commemorations, inaugural addresses)

  • Deliberative — Political/policy-oriented, future-focused

  • Forensic — Judicial, addressing past actions

  • Polemical — Confrontational, calls to action

  • Sermonic — Moral/spiritual exhortation

1. Speaker positioning:

** **How the speaker presents themselves as credible, moral, emotional, authoritative, relatable, or representative.

Useful metalanguage:

  • ethos

  • persona

  • moral authority

  • credibility

  • shared identity

  • self-positioning

  • collective voice

Example analytical sentence: The speaker constructs ethos by presenting themselves not merely as an individual, but as a representative voice for a wider community.

2. Audience relationship

This is very important for speeches. Students should always ask: How does the speaker build a relationship with the audience?

Useful metalanguage:

  • direct address: create an immediate connection

  • inclusive pronouns: a sense of unity

  • collective identity: we are a nation of xxx, united

  • shared values, common moral beliefs

  • audience positioning: you are xxx, position the audience as active agents

  • call to action :CTA, transform emotion to concrete actions

  • rhetorical questions: pressures audiences

  • imperatives: create the forceful tone

  • \ Hypophora — Posing and answering one’s own question*

  • \ Apostrophe — Addressing absent person/abstract concept*

  • \ Anecdote — Personal story for relatability and pathos*

Example: The repeated use of “we” positions the audience as active participants rather than passive listeners, creating a sense of shared responsibility.

3. Persuasive appeals

  • Ethos — Establishment of credibility, moral authority, shared identity (“As a mother, as a citizen…”)

  • Pathos — Emotional appeal through imagery, anecdote, loaded diction

  • Logos — Logical appeal via statistics, evidence, reasoning, cause-and-effect structures

4. Structure of argument

  • opening hook

  • capture

  • contextualization

  • provide

  • problem presentation

  • development of evidence

  • emotional escalation

  • repetition

  • deepens audience sympathy

  • shift / turning point

  • from anger to hope

  • from descripton to analysis

  • climax / emotional peak

  • final appeal

  • call to action

5. Rhetorical & Stylistic Devices

Schemes (structural/syntactic):

  • Anaphora — Repetition at beginnings (e.g., “I have a dream…”)

  • Epistrophe — Repetition at ends of clauses

  • Parallelism — Symmetrical grammatical structures

  • Tricolon / rule of three — Three parallel elements (“of the people, by the people, for the people”)

  • Antithesis — Balanced contrast (“Ask not what your country can do for you…”)

  • *Chiasmus — Inverted parallelism

  • *Asyndeton / Polysyndeton — Omission or piling of conjunctions for pace/emphasis

  • Climax / anticlimax — Ascending or descending intensity

Tropes (figurative):

  • Metaphor & extended metaphor — Often central to a speech’s controlling idea

  • Hyperbole / understatement

  • Allusion — To scripture, history, founding documents (builds shared cultural memory)

  • Personification (e.g., of nation, justice, freedom)

  • *Synecdoche / metonymy

Example: The anaphora creates a rhythmic accumulation of grievance, making the argument feel urgent, collective, and emotionally unavoidable.

6. Diction & Tone

  • Register — Formal, colloquial, elevated, intimate

  • Inclusive pronouns — “We,” “us,” “our” → constructs collective identity

  • Modality — Modal verbs (“must,” “shall,” “will”) signal certainty, obligation, vision

  • Imperatives — Direct calls to action

  • Loaded / emotive diction — Connotative word choices

  • *Euphemism / dysphemism

  • *Lexical fields — Recurring semantic clusters (e.g., warfare, light/dark, journey)

7. Paralinguistic / Performative Features /Speech-specific sound and performance

Useful speech-specific focus:

  • rhythm

  • cadence

  • repetition

  • sentence length

  • punctuation

  • pauses

  • short declarative sentences

  • climactic build-up

  • Volume modulation, if included

  • applause cues, if included (often engineered through tricolons or antithesis）

  • stage directions, if included

Paper 1 Annotation: FDR’s Speech (Excerpt)

Contextual & Situational Elements

  • Speaker: President Franklin D. Roosevelt — sitting head of state, commander-in-chief

  • Audience: Immediate — the 77th Congress; Secondary — the American public (still largely isolationist) and, indirectly, foreign powers (Britain, the Axis).

  • Occasion / Kairos: January 6, 1941 — Europe under Nazi advance, Britain alone; delivered as the constitutionally mandated State of the Union.

  • Purpose: To persuade Congress and the American public to abandon isolationism and support intervention/aid to democracies abroad.

  • Medium: Live political address before Congress, also broadcast nationally by radio.

  • Genre Convention: Primarily deliberative, with sermonic undertones

Annotated Text

Mr. President, Mr. Speaker, Members of the Seventy-seventh Congress: [1]

I address you, the Members of the Seventy-seventh Congress, at a moment unprecedented in the history of the Union [2]. I use the word “unprecedented,” [3] because at no previous time has American security been as seriously threatened from without as it is today.

Since the permanent formation of our Government under the Constitution, in 1789 [4], most of the periods of crisis in our history have related to our domestic affairs. Fortunately, only one of these—the four-year War Between the States [5]—ever threatened our national unity. Today, thank God [6], one hundred and thirty million Americans, in forty-eight States [7], have forgotten points of the compass in our national unity [8].

It is true that prior to 1914 the United States often had been disturbed by events in other Continents. We had even engaged in two wars with European nations and in a number of undeclared wars in the West Indies, in the Mediterranean and in the Pacific [9] for the maintenance of American rights and for the principles of peaceful commerce. But in no case had a serious threat been raised against our national safety or our continued independence.

What I seek to convey is the historic truth that the United States as a nation has at all times maintained clear, definite opposition [10], to any attempt to lock us in behind an ancient Chinese wall [11] while the procession of civilization [12] went past. Today, thinking of our children and of their children [13], we oppose enforced isolation for ourselves or for any other part of the Americas.

That determination of ours, extending over all these years, was proved, for example, during the quarter century of wars following the French Revolution.

While the Napoleonic struggles did threaten interests of the United States because of the French foothold in the West Indies and in Louisiana, and while we engaged in the War of 1812 to vindicate our right to peaceful trade, it is nevertheless clear that neither France nor Great Britain, nor any other nation, was aiming at domination of the whole world [14].

In like fashion from 1815 to 1914– ninety-nine years [15]– no single war in Europe or in Asia constituted a real threat against our future or against the future of any other American nation.

Except in the Maximilian interlude in Mexico, no foreign power sought to establish itself in this Hemisphere; and the strength of the British fleet in the Atlantic has been a friendly strength. It is still a friendly strength. [16]

Even when the World War broke out in 1914, it seemed to contain only small threat of danger to our own American future. But, as time went on, the American people began to visualize what the downfall of democratic nations might mean to our own democracy [17].

We need not overemphasize imperfections in the Peace of Versailles. We need not harp on failure of the democracies to deal with problems of world reconstruction. We should remember [18] that the Peace of 1919 was far less unjust than the kind of “pacification” [19] which began even before Munich, and which is being carried on under the new order of tyranny that seeks to spread over every continent today [20]. The American people have unalterably set their faces against that tyranny [21].

Every realist knows [22] that the democratic way of life is at this moment being directly assailed in every part of the world–assailed either by arms, or by secret spreading of poisonous propaganda [23] by those who seek to destroy unity and promote discord [24] in nations that are still at peace.

During sixteen long months [25] this assault has blotted out the whole pattern of democratic life [26] in an appalling number of independent nations, great and small [27]. The assailants are still on the march [28], threatening other nations, great and small.

Therefore, as your President, performing my constitutional duty [29] to “give to the Congress information of the state of the Union,” [30] I find it, unhappily [31], necessary to report that the future and the safety of our country and of our democracy [32] are overwhelmingly involved in events far beyond our borders [33].

Analysis Table

# Quoted Element Genre Device / Authorial Choice Effect & Purpose
1 “Mr. President, Mr. Speaker, Members of the Seventy-seventh Congress” Formal salutation / deliberative genre convention Opens the speech in line with the conventions of a State of the Union address; the formal register signals institutional respect and establishes FDR’s ethos through proper acknowledgement of constitutional offices. + audience
2 “at a moment unprecedented in the history of the Union” Opening hook + appeal to kairos Foregrounds the occasion as historically singular, immediately framing the speech’s urgency and justifying the deliberative shift away from past policy.
3 “unprecedented” Repetition / loaded diction Drawing explicit attention to his own word choice makes “unprecedented” a keyword of the speech; the emotive diction primes the audience to accept that conventional isolationist responses are inadequate.
4 “the Constitution, in 1789” Allusion (to founding documents) Builds shared cultural memory and reinforces ethos by anchoring the argument in the legitimacy of the nation’s founding.
5 “War Between the States” Euphemism for the Civil War The softened phrasing minimises divisiveness, supporting his collective identity message; recalls an internal threat that was overcome, modelling future unity.
6 “thank God” Sermonic register / loaded diction The religious interjection introduces a sermonic tone and constructs shared values with a predominantly Christian audience, strengthening pathos.
7 “one hundred and thirty million Americans, in forty-eight States” Logos through statistics The precise figures lend factual weight to the abstract claim of unity; appeals to logos while simultaneously evoking scale and collective strength.
8 “forgotten points of the compass in our national unity” Metaphor Regional divisions (N/S/E/W) are figuratively erased, reinforcing collective identity and constructing the audience as a unified nation facing an external threat.
9 “the West Indies, in the Mediterranean and in the Pacific” Tricolon / geographical accumulation The triadic listing produces rhythmic cadence while supplying logos: historical evidence that America has never truly been isolationist.
10 “clear, definite opposition” Parallelism / loaded diction Twin emphatic adjectives produce a tone of conviction and certainty, reinforcing FDR’s moral authority.
11 “lock us in behind an ancient Chinese wall” Allusion + metaphor The image of the Great Wall casts isolationism as archaic and culturally foreign; positions intervention as inherently American and forward-looking.
12 “procession of civilization” Personification Civilisation is personified as a moving parade, implying that isolation means being left behind — a teleological framing of history that supports his deliberative argument.
13 “thinking of our children and of their children” Pathos / shared values The generational image activates parental responsibility and frames foreign policy as a moral question of legacy, fostering shared identity across generations.
14 “neither France nor Great Britain, nor any other nation, was aiming at domination of the whole world” Antithesis (implicit) Sets up an unstated contrast with the present Axis powers, allowing FDR to characterise today’s enemy without naming it — a calculated self-positioning as measured rather than warmongering.
15 “1815 to 1914– ninety-nine years” Logos / precise temporal data Specific dates supply credibility and historical authority, framing the present crisis as exceptional by quantifiable contrast.
16 “friendly strength… It is still a friendly strength.” Epistrophe / repetition The repeated closing phrase produces cadence and reinforces the Anglo-American alliance; the short declarative sentence (“It is still a friendly strength.”) delivers a forceful, uncompromising affirmation.
17 “began to visualize what the downfall of democratic nations might mean to our own democracy” Lexical field of democracy + cause-and-effect reasoning The recurring lexical field (“democratic,” “democracy”) binds foreign and domestic fates; the verb “visualize” creates audience positioning as imaginatively engaged participants.
18 “We need not overemphasize… We need not harp… We should remember” Anaphora + tricolon + modality The triadic opening produces rhythmic climactic build-up; the modal verbs (“need not,” “should”) guide audience reasoning, marking a shift / turning point from past grievance toward present moral clarity.
19 “pacification” Dysphemism via scare quotes / irony The quotation marks expose totalitarian language as deceptive doublespeak; reframes the enemy’s register as morally bankrupt.
20 “the new order of tyranny that seeks to spread over every continent today” Loaded diction + lexical field of disease/contagion “Spread” connotes infection; “tyranny” carries moral revulsion. The lexical field of contagion casts fascism as a global pathology requiring intervention — a clear problem presentation.
21 “unalterably set their faces against that tyranny” Allusion (Biblical) + personification The scriptural cadence (cf. Isaiah 50:7) blends civic and sermonic registers, elevating American resistance to a near-sacred mission and consolidating moral authority.
22 “Every realist knows” Audience positioning / rhetorical presupposition Frames disagreement as naïveté — any listener wishing to be seen as a “realist” must accept the premise. A subtle call to action that pressures isolationists into compliance.
23 “directly assailed in every part of the world–assailed either by arms, or by secret spreading of poisonous propaganda” Repetition + lexical field of warfare/poison {diacope} The repeated “assailed” intensifies threat; the lexical field of weaponry and toxicity portrays ideology itself as a weapon, sustaining the contagion imagery from [20].
24 “destroy unity and promote discord” Antithesis The balanced opposition crystallises a moral binary: the enemy’s goal is the inverse of American shared values established in [8].
25 “sixteen long months” Loaded / emotive diction The adjective “long” transforms a neutral time-span into emotional duration, evoking exhaustion and urgency — clear pathos.
26 “blotted out the whole pattern of democratic life” Metaphor (erasure/extinguishing) “Blotted out” suggests violent obliteration of ink or light, presenting democracy as fragile and erasable — an emotional escalation that heightens stakes.
27 “great and small” Antithesis The polar pairing encompasses totality, suggesting that no nation, regardless of size, is safe — universalising the threat.
28 “The assailants are still on the march” Metaphor + present continuous tense The ongoing tense conveys imminence and momentum; the depersonalising noun “assailants” casts the enemy as faceless and predatory, building climactic build-up.
29 “as your President, performing my constitutional duty” Ethos / self-positioning FDR frames the speech not as personal advocacy but as legal obligation, deflecting accusations of warmongering and reinforcing credibility through his institutional role.
30 “‘give to the Congress information of the state of the Union,’” Allusion (direct quotation from the Constitution) Embedding the literal foundational text anchors his words in supreme civic authority and confirms genre conventions of the deliberative address.
31 “unhappily” Loaded / emotive diction The adverb signals reluctance, constructing a persona of a reluctant statesman acting out of duty rather than ambition — pre-empts opposition.
32 “the future and the safety of our country and of our democracy” Polysyndeton + lexical field of democracy The repeated “and” piles up stakes — nation, security, democratic system itself — producing rhythmic climactic build-up toward the speech’s central claim.
33 “overwhelmingly involved in events far beyond our borders” Final appeal / thesis statement The closing sentence delivers FDR’s core argument: isolation is no longer possible. The adverb “overwhelmingly” leaves no rhetorical space for dissent, completing the dismantling of isolationism begun at [11] and functioning as the excerpt’s final appeal.

Thesis & Intro

Suggested Thesis (matching with our body paragraph core ideas)

Delivering a deliberative address at a moment of national crisis, FDR shifts his audience’s stance on intervention through a tripartite rhetorical construction: he first forges a reluctant constitutional persona that grounds his authority in founding legitimacy, then positions his audience as a unified generational collective bound by shared values and posterity, and finally mobilises an escalating lexical field of contagion and assault that reframes a foreign-policy question as an existential threat to democracy itself.

PART B — Visual Metalanguage

B1. Composition & Layout

  • Rule of thirds — key elements at grid intersections create dynamic focus.

  • Focal point — the eye’s first landing place; achieved via contrast, scale, lighting, isolation.

  • Visual path (Z-pattern / F-pattern) — directs reading order; controls the sequence of meaning.

  • Symmetry vs. asymmetry — stability/trust vs. energy/disruption.

  • White space (negative space) — signals luxury, clarity, sophistication; isolates and elevates the product.

  • Framing — what is included/excluded; tight crops create intimacy, wide shots create context.

  • Foreground / midground / background — layered hierarchy of importance.

B2. Color

  • Palette choice — warm (energy, urgency, appetite) vs. cool (calm, trust, technology).

  • Brand colors — instant recognition (e.g., red = Coca-Cola, blue = Facebook).

  • Color contrast — draws attention to CTA or product.

  • Monochrome / desaturation — seriousness, nostalgia, gravity (common in PSAs, charity ads).

  • Saturation & vibrancy — emotional intensity.

B3. Typography

  • Serif → tradition, authority, luxury.

  • Sans-serif → modernity, clarity, accessibility.

  • Script / handwritten → intimacy, craft, authenticity.

  • Display / bold → urgency, command, headline impact.

  • Hierarchy — size and weight signal what to read first.

  • Kerning, tracking, leading — density vs. openness of feel.

B4. Human Figures

  • The gaze — direct address (demand: engages viewer) vs. averted (offer: invites observation).

  • Body language & gesture — confidence, vulnerability, aspiration.

  • Facial expression — anchors emotional appeal.

  • Point of view / camera angle —

  • Low angle → power, aspiration

  • High angle → vulnerability, smallness

  • Eye level → equality, intimacy

  • POV shot → places viewer inside the experience.

  • Social distance — close-up (intimate) vs. medium (social) vs. long shot (public).

B5. Image Type & Style

  • Photographic realism vs. illustration / stylization vs. CGI / surreal.

  • Documentary aesthetic — authenticity, often in PSAs.

  • Hyper-stylized / glossy — aspiration, luxury.

  • Juxtaposition — placing unlike images together for shock or insight.

PART C — Linguistic & Textual Metalanguage (the major missing layer)

C1. Structural Text Elements of an Ad

Element Function
Headline The verbal hook — often the loudest persuasive claim.
Subheadline / deck Bridges headline to body; clarifies or extends.
Body copy Builds the argument, evidence, narrative.
Slogan / tagline Distilled brand essence, designed for memorability.
Call to action (CTA) Imperative urging behaviour (“Buy now,” “Donate today,” “Just do it”).
Logo / wordmark Brand signature; closes the visual sentence.
Disclaimer / fine print Legal hedging; ironically, often where truth lives.

C2. Diction & Word Choice

  • Register — formal/informal, technical/colloquial, intimate/authoritative.

  • Lexical fields — clusters of words building a theme (e.g., purity, freshness, nature for skincare).

  • Connotation vs. denotation — emotional vs. literal weight.

  • Loaded language — emotionally charged terms.

  • Buzzwords / jargon — signals expertise or trendiness.

  • Sensory diction — appeals to taste, touch, smell, sound, sight.

C3. Syntax & Grammar

  • Imperatives — “Discover.” “Taste.” “Believe.” → command and intimacy.

  • Second-person pronouns (“you,” “your”) — direct address; constructs the reader as already a user.

  • Inclusive “we” / “us” — community, shared identity.

  • Short, fragmented sentences — punch, memorability, mimicking thought.

  • Elliptical structures — omitted words create speed and confidence.

  • Parallelism — rhythm and balance.

  • Modality — high modality (“will,” “must”) = certainty; low modality (“may,” “could”) = subtlety.

  • Present tense — immediacy, timelessness.

C4. Rhetorical & Literary Devices

  • Metaphor / simile — reframes the product through another concept.

  • Personification — gives the product agency.

  • Hyperbole — exaggerated claims.

  • Pun / double meaning / wordplay — wit and memorability.

  • Alliteration, assonance, rhyme — phonetic stickiness (key in slogans).

  • Allusion — cultural, literary, or historical reference building shared knowledge.

  • Rhetorical question — invites internal agreement.

  • Antithesis / juxtaposition — sharpens contrast.

  • Tricolon — rhythmic three-part list.

  • Anaphora — repetition for emphasis.

  • Irony / satire — common in subvertisements and edgy brand voice.

C5. Persuasive Appeals (Aristotelian, applied to ads)

  • Ethos — brand reputation, expert endorsement, celebrity, heritage claims (“Since 1886”).

  • Pathos — emotional storytelling, music, imagery of family/loss/joy.

  • Logos — statistics, comparisons, scientific claims, demonstrations.

  • Kairos — timeliness; ads tied to a cultural moment, season, or crisis.

PART D — Multimodal Interaction (how image + text co-create meaning)

Concept Definition Analytical Move
Anchorage Text fixes the meaning of an ambiguous image. “The caption disambiguates the visual, directing the viewer toward…”
Relay Text and image work complementarily, neither complete alone. “Image and copy operate in dialogue, with meaning suspended between them…”
Reinforcement Text restates what the image shows. Creates emphasis, certainty.
Contradiction / subversion Text and image clash deliberately. Generates irony, humour, critique — common in PSAs and satire.
Sequencing Visual path determines order of decoding. Trace the reader’s journey through the ad.
Hierarchy of modes Which mode dominates? Visual-led or text-led? Signals genre and audience (luxury = visual; PSA = often text-driven).

PART E — Contextual & Strategic Layer

E1. Audience Construction

  • Target demographic — age, gender, class, region, lifestyle.

  • Psychographic — values, aspirations, anxieties the ad activates.

  • Positioning the reader — as aspirational future-self, as guilty bystander (charity), as savvy insider (luxury), as concerned citizen (PSA).

  • Assumed shared knowledge — cultural references, brand familiarity.

E2. Brand & Commercial Context

  • Brand voice — playful, authoritative, rebellious, caring.

  • Market positioning — premium vs. value vs. disruptive.

  • Competitive differentiation — what claim distinguishes this brand?

  • Campaign context — single ad or part of a series?

E3. Ideology & Values

  • Encoded values — what worldview does the ad normalize? (success, individualism, family, sustainability, beauty standards).

  • Representation — gender, race, class, body type, age — who is visible, who is absent.

  • Cultural assumptions — what must the viewer already believe for the ad to work?

PART F — Subtype-Specific Conventions

F1. Commercial Product Ad

  • Foregrounds product + aspiration; high-gloss aesthetic; CTA toward purchase.

F2. Public Service Announcement (PSA)

  • Shock, guilt, or empathy as primary appeal.

  • Often desaturated palette, stark typography, heavy negative space.

  • Subverts ad conventions to disrupt complacency.

  • CTA toward behaviour change, not purchase.

F3. Charity / NGO Appeal

  • Pathos-dominant; direct gaze of vulnerable subject.

  • Statistics paired with individual story (logos + pathos).

  • CTA toward donation / action.

F4. Luxury Ad

  • Minimalism, white space, restrained typography.

  • Often no explicit CTA — sells aura, not product.

  • Aspirational gaze; aestheticized lifestyle.

F5. Political / Advocacy Ad

  • High modality, identity-laden lexis, us vs. them structures.

  • Symbolic imagery (flags, landscapes, faces).

F6. Satirical / Subvertisement

  • Parodies ad conventions to critique consumerism or a specific brand.

  • Relies on viewer’s genre literacy — irony depends on recognising the form being mocked.

PART G — Analytical Sentence Frames (for essay writing)

Use these to push from identification to analysis:

  • “The ad positions the viewer as [X] through its use of [device], constructing a relationship of [Y].”

  • “The interplay between [visual element] and [textual element] creates an anchorage that directs the reader toward [interpretation].”

  • “By foregrounding [focal point] against [negative space / contrasting element], the composition elevates the product from object to [symbol of X].”

  • “The lexical field of [X] in the body copy reinforces the visual register of [Y], fusing [product] with [abstract value].”

  • “The direct gaze of the subject converts the viewer from observer to addressee, mobilising pathos in service of [purpose].”

  • “The ad’s adherence to [subtype convention] — and its strategic departure at [moment] — generates [effect].”
