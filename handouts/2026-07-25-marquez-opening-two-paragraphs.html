Paper 1 Speech Annotation Handout

Gabriel Garcia Marquez, Nobel Lecture excerpt, "The Solitude of Latin America"

Guiding question: How does Marquez use history, imagery, and collective voice to explain the "solitude" and identity of Latin America?

What To Memorize For Speech Analysis

Paper 1 formula: authorial choice -> effect -> purpose -> audience. Do not stop at naming the device.

Speech framework: 0 contextual & situational elements, then 1 speaker positioning, 2 audience relationship, 3 persuasive appeals, 4 structure of argument, 5 rhetorical & stylistic devices, 6 diction/tone and speech-specific rhythm.

For this speech: Marquez argues that Latin America seems "fantastic" because its real history is so extreme that ordinary language struggles to make it believable.

0. Contextual & Situational Elements

Speaker: Gabriel Garcia Marquez, Colombian novelist and Nobel laureate.

Audience: Swedish Academy / European literary audience / global readers.

Occasion: Nobel Lecture, 8 December 1982.

Purpose: To explain Latin America's identity and ask the audience to understand its reality on its own terms.

Genre

Main genre: Nobel lecture / ceremonial speech.

Also contains: cultural manifesto, historical testimony, political critique.

Key move: Marquez begins with literature, then turns the Nobel audience toward history, suffering, and identity.

Annotated Speech Excerpt

The Solitude of Latin America

1 Antonio Pigafetta, a Florentine navigator who went with Magellan on the first voyage around the world 5 Historical anecdote / exemplum, wrote, upon his passage through our southern lands of America11 Speaker, a strictly accurate account that nonetheless resembles a venture into fantasy25 Devices. In it he recorded impossible animals and a Patagonian "giant" terrified by his own image in a mirror5 Catalogue/imagery.

2 This short and fascinating book, which even then contained the seeds of our present-day novels31 Speaker5 Metaphor, is by no means the most staggering account of our reality. The Chronicles of the Indies left us countless others. Eldorado, the fountain of eternal youth, Atahualpa's ransom, and gold-filled landscapes appear as examples of colonial myth becoming historical reality45 Allusion. One founder's lust for gold beset us until recently54 Structure.

3 Our independence from Spanish domination did not put us beyond the reach of madness64 Structure. Marquez then lists rulers who held a funeral for a lost leg, seated a corpse on a presidential chair, used a pendulum to detect poison, and tried to defeat scarlet fever with red paper on streetlamps5 Grotesque catalogue. The comic strangeness becomes a critique of political absurdity and dictatorship76 Diction.

4 Eleven years ago, Pablo Neruda enlightened this audience with his word. Since then, the Europeans of good will - and sometimes those of bad, as well82 Audience have been struck by the unearthly tidings of Latin America5 Mythic imagery. But Marquez shifts sharply: We have not had a moment's rest94 Structure. He then names wars, coups, ethnocide, infant deaths, disappearances, imprisoned mothers, and mass political death5 Accumulation.

5 Twenty million Latin American children died before the age of one103 Appeals. Those missing because of repression number nearly one hundred and twenty thousand, as if no one could account for all the inhabitants of Uppsala112 Audience. If this had happened in the United States, the corresponding figure would be one million six hundred thousand violent deaths in four years5 Analogy. One million people have fled Chile; Uruguay has lost one out of every five citizens; El Salvador produces almost one refugee every twenty minutes 5 Statistical accumulation.

6 I dare to think that it is this outsized reality, and not just its literary expression124 Structure, that has deserved the attention of the Swedish Academy of Letters. A reality not of paper lives within Latin Americans and nourishes creativity. Poets and beggars, musicians and prophets, warriors and scoundrels131 Speaker have had to ask little of imagination, because the problem is a lack of conventional means to render our lives believable. This, my friends, is the crux of our solitude144 Structure.

Rhetorical & Stylistic Devices Map

Use this to study Dimension 5. You do not need to write about every device in an essay; choose the devices that best support your argument.

Device / ChoiceExample In The SpeechEffect
Historical anecdote / exemplumThe opening story from Pigafetta's travel account.Works as a narrative hook and a concrete example that introduces the speech's central idea: Latin American reality can sound like fantasy.
Historical allusionPigafetta, Magellan, Eldorado, Atahualpa, Neruda.Gives the speech cultural authority and places Latin America inside a long history of European encounter and misreading.
Paradox"Strictly accurate" but like "fantasy."Introduces the central tension: Latin American reality appears unbelievable to outsiders.
Catalogue / enumerationAnimals, colonial myths, strange rulers, wars, deaths, exiles.Creates abundance and accumulation, making Latin America's reality feel overwhelming.
Grotesque imageryFuneral for a lost leg; corpse seated on a presidential chair; red-paper streetlamps.Blends comedy and horror to expose political absurdity.
Metaphor"Seeds of our present-day novels"; "crux of our solitude."Turns history into the origin of literature and gives the final idea a memorable shape.
Mythic / magical diction"Unearthly tidings," "haunted men," "historic women."Creates a legendary tone while preparing the audience to see that the legend is grounded in reality.
Loaded / emotive diction"Madness," "ethnocide," "repression," "ill-fated," "violent deaths."Gives the historical evidence moral force and prevents the audience from reading the suffering neutrally.
ContrastFantasy vs accuracy; literary expression vs outsized reality; sorrow vs beauty.Shows the double nature of Latin American experience: imaginative and historical, creative and painful.
Statistical accumulationTwenty million children, 120,000 missing, one refugee every twenty minutes.Uses logos to intensify pathos; suffering becomes measurable and difficult to dismiss.
Analogy / comparisonUppsala, United States, Norway.Translates Latin American suffering into terms the European audience can understand.
Aphoristic closure"This, my friends, is the crux of our solitude."Compresses the speech's argument into a final memorable thesis.
